# Lesson: highland cow

The step pictures are in steps/, and each step beside the one before it in pairs/.

## Before you start

You're about to paint a shaggy Highland cow standing in tall summer grass at sunset, with violet hills and a gold sky behind it. The heart of it is the cow's face: the glowing fringe, the soft nose and the two curved horns.

The light: The light is the low sun, sitting behind the cow near the hills. It makes the sky glow gold, leaves most of the cow's coat in shade, and catches a bright gold rim along the fringe, the sunlit side of the cow and the grass tips.

What this painting teaches:
- Thick to thin (steps 14, 15, 21): Press the full width of the angle brush, then turn onto its edge and lighten as you pull, so each mark tapers to a point. It builds the cow's whole shaggy coat, lock by lock, and its gold rim. Leave gaps so the darker coat shows between the locks.
- Flicking (steps 18, 19, 20): A quick stroke up from the base of a blade, lifting off so the tip thins. It makes the tall grass and lets it grow over the cow's legs. Keep the brush lightly loaded, since too much paint makes flat blobs, and let each lighter pass thin out as it goes.
- Dry brush (steps 2, 3, 5, 22): Only the tip of a dry brush goes in the paint, skimmed lightly so the marks break up and catch the canvas texture. It lays the cloud streaks, the soft hill shadows and the last bright lights. If it goes on solid, the brush was too loaded.

The way there: First you blend the sky and dry-brush its clouds, because everything else sits on it. Next come the far hills and the sliver of loch, then the rolling hillside and the meadow, so the whole canvas is covered before the cow arrives. The cow is built the way a teacher draws it: a flat shape, its shadow, then the horns. Then its coat goes on lock by lock, light over dark, and the face comes after that. The tall grass is flicked on next so it grows over the cow's legs, and the gold rim and brightest lights go on last.

This stage, The sky: This stage covers the canvas with a smooth sunset sky and its gold cloud streaks.

## Step 1: Blend the sunset sky
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/01.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/01.jpg)
Paints:
- Sky blue (#8eb8e1): 2 parts white, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
- Warm gold (#fab949): 2 parts yellow, 2 parts white, 1 part red
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. Start: Start with Sky blue at the top edge of the canvas, stroking side to side across the full width.
2. Strokes: Work down in long side-to-side strokes. Without rinsing, pick up Warm gold and paint it in, then stroke back up over the join several times while both are wet.
3. Where the paint sits: The blue band is about 5 1/4 in tall, starting at the top edge. Warm gold fills everything from there to the bottom edge, since hills and land go over it later.
4. Leave: Leave no white canvas anywhere. A few soft, deeper orange streaks in the gold can stay; you don't need to smooth them all away.
5. Stop when: The whole canvas is covered: clear blue at the top edge melting into warm gold with no hard line. If a line shows, add more gold at the join and blend again.

This stage, The sky: This stage covers the canvas with a smooth sunset sky and its gold cloud streaks.

## Step 2: Dry-brush the gold cloud streaks
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/02.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/02.jpg)
Paints:
- Warm gold (#fab949): 2 parts yellow, 2 parts white, 1 part red
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the sky dry completely, or the dry brush will drag the wet colours together.
2. Start: Start near the top right corner of the canvas, laying the longest streak first and carrying it across toward the left.
3. As in step 1: This is the same Warm gold as the sky, but now on a dry brush, skimmed on in broken streaks instead of blended.
4. Strokes: Skim the dry brush sideways in long, light strokes that drop gently toward the left. Then dab a few small broken puffs into the blue.
5. Where the paint sits: The clouds sit where the blue meets the gold, most of it on the right side, toward the sun. Their middle is about 3 1/2 in from the top edge.
6. Leave: Keep the strokes broken so blue sky shows through and between them. Leave the lower sky alone.
7. Stop when: Ragged, flat gold streaks cross the blue, thickest toward the right edge, with sky peeking through. If one went on solid, let it dry before going on.

This stage, The sky: This stage covers the canvas with a smooth sunset sky and its gold cloud streaks. By its end: The canvas is all sky now, clear blue melting into warm gold, with ragged lit cloud streaks.

## Step 3: Light the tops of the clouds
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/03.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/03.jpg)
Paints:
- Cream gold (#cebd9b): 1 part white, a touch of yellow, a touch of red, a touch of ultramarine
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the gold cloud streaks from step 2 dry first.
2. Start: Start on the cloud streaks near the left edge of the canvas, along their upper edges.
3. As in step 2: Same dry brush and same skimming touch as the cloud streaks, but now a paler paint, only along each streak's lit edge.
4. Strokes: Skim the dry brush lightly side to side along the upper edge of each streak, in a few soft passes. Build it up slowly rather than pressing.
5. Where the paint sits: The light sits only along the upper edges of the streaks, most of it on the left side. The rest of each streak keeps its deeper gold.
6. Leave: Leave the darker streak colour showing through the light, and the blue sky around the clouds untouched.
7. Stop when: The clouds look lit along their upper edges with a soft paler glow, a gentle change rather than new solid shapes. The sky is finished.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: This stage lays in the distant violet hills, shades the nearer range, and adds the pale sliver of loch.

## Step 4: Lay in the far violet hills
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/04.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/04.jpg)
Paints:
- Pale violet (#7381be): 4 parts white, 3 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna
- Blue-violet (#5970b1): 1 part ultramarine, 1 part white, a touch of burnt sienna
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the sky and clouds dry before painting the hills over them.
2. Start: Start with Pale violet along the far ridge, its top about 2 1/4 in from the top edge, painting a gently bumpy line of peaks across the full width.
3. Strokes: Keep the brush level and pull long strokes left and right. Then switch to Blue-violet and carry it down in level bands, each edge waving slightly like a ridge.
4. Where the paint sits: Pale violet stays a thin band along the peaks, since far hills fade in haze. Blue-violet fills the rest down to the bottom edge, as a base for the land.
5. Leave: Leave the sky and clouds untouched over the hilltops; paint right up to the peaks and stop.
6. Stop when: Violet covers the canvas from the peaks to the bottom edge, paler along the far ridge, with soft wavy ridge lines. No shading yet.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: This stage lays in the distant violet hills, shades the nearer range, and adds the pale sliver of loch.

## Step 5: Shade the near hills
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/05.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/05.jpg)
Paints:
- Blue-violet (#5970b1): 1 part ultramarine, 1 part white, a touch of burnt sienna
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the violet hills from step 4 dry first.
2. Start: Start at the right edge of the canvas, about 3 1/2 in from the top edge, just past the pale far ridge.
3. As in step 4: The same Blue-violet as the nearer hills, but now dry-brushed in light, broken passes rather than laid on in level bands.
4. Strokes: Skim the dry brush sideways along the nearer ridge, following its slope as it climbs toward the left, in several light passes.
5. Where the paint sits: The dark sits on the nearer ridge's slopes, in shade from the low sun, so the near range separates from the pale far one.
6. Leave: Leave the pale far ridge and the sky alone, and keep the lower violet bands as they are.
7. Stop when: A soft, darker band of ridge crosses the hills and reaches the right edge, feathery rather than solid. The two ranges now read apart.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: This stage lays in the distant violet hills, shades the nearer range, and adds the pale sliver of loch. By its end: Two ranges of violet hills sit against the sunset, the nearer one darker, with a thin silver strip of loch tucked against them.

## Step 6: Lay in the sliver of loch
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/06.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/06.jpg)
Paints:
- Loch silver (#d4cdbc): 12 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part yellow, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: #6 flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hill shadows from step 5 dry first.
2. Start: Start at the right edge of the canvas, tucked right against the dark ridge from step 5.
3. As in step 4: Level strokes again, as for the hills, but with a small #6 flat and only a thin strip of water.
4. Strokes: Keep the brush level and pull short, flat strokes from the right edge toward the left, tapering the strip to a point.
5. Where the paint sits: The water is a thin wedge about 1/2 in tall, its left end about 9 3/4 in from the left edge. Keep it flat and pale; it is too small for ripples.
6. Leave: Leave the dark ridge crisp where it meets the water, and the hills around it untouched.
7. Stop when: A thin, pale silver strip of water sits at the right edge of the canvas, tapering to a point, flat and calm.

This stage, The hillside and meadow: This stage builds the rolling green hillside and the meadow, so the whole canvas is covered before the cow.

## Step 7: Lay in the green hillside
Part of: The hillside and meadow
Picture: steps/07.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/07.jpg)
Paints:
- Olive green (#807e2f): 3 parts yellow, 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hills and the loch dry first.
2. Start: Start near the left edge of the canvas, about 3 1/2 in from the top edge, where the hillside's line begins.
3. As in step 4: Long strokes again, like the hills, but with the big 1 inch flat, slanting with the land and leaving gaps between bands.
4. Strokes: Pull long strokes that slant gently down toward the right, following the roll of the land. Keep their ends broken and bumpy, band after band, down to the bottom edge.
5. Where the paint sits: Lay the paint in loose bands with gaps between them, so the hillside looks striped by rolling ground. The bands cover about half the area.
6. Leave: Leave long purple stripes from step 4 showing between the bands; step 8 fills them. Keep clear of the loch, the dark ridge and the far hills.
7. Stop when: Slanting olive bands run from the hills to the bottom edge, with purple showing between them like furrows. It looks striped and unfinished, not covered.

This stage, The hillside and meadow: This stage builds the rolling green hillside and the meadow, so the whole canvas is covered before the cow.

## Step 8: Streak the dark patches on the hillside
Part of: The hillside and meadow
Picture: steps/08.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/08.jpg)
Paints:
- Deep green (#53613f): 3 parts ultramarine, 2 parts yellow, 1 part red, a touch of white
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the green bands from step 7 dry first.
2. Start: Start at the hillside's far line, about 3 1/4 in from the top edge, near the left edge of the canvas, filling the first purple stripe.
3. As in step 7: The same slanting strokes as the green bands, with a smaller 1/2 inch flat, this time filling the gaps between them.
4. Strokes: Pull short horizontal strokes along each purple gap, following the same slant down toward the right as the bands from step 7. Let the ends break into little bumps.
5. Where the paint sits: Fill every purple stripe down to the bottom edge, so the dark becomes the shaded dips of the land. Also darken the far slope left of the loch.
6. Leave: Leave the bands from step 7 showing as the sunny ridges; overlap their edges only slightly.
7. Stop when: No purple is left on the hillside. It reads as rolling land in stripes of light and dark green, sloping down toward the right.

This stage, The hillside and meadow: This stage builds the rolling green hillside and the meadow, so the whole canvas is covered before the cow. By its end: Striped green hillside rolls back toward the hills, and a textured green-and-gold meadow fills the front of the canvas.

## Step 9: Lay in the meadow
Part of: The hillside and meadow
Picture: steps/09.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/09.jpg)
Paints:
- Olive green (#807e2f): 3 parts yellow, 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine
- Yellow-green (#9f8f30): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hillside from step 8 dry first.
2. Start: Start along the meadow's ragged far edge, about 7 1/4 in from the top edge of the canvas, working across from the left edge.
3. As in step 7: Olive green again, as for the hillside, but now with Yellow-green on the same brush, in short criss-cross strokes.
4. Strokes: Load both greens and paint short criss-cross X strokes in two directions. Work down to the bottom edge, letting the strokes stand slightly upright like grass.
5. Where the paint sits: Keep more Yellow-green along the meadow's far edge, where the low sun skims the grass, and more Olive green lower down. The two blend as the strokes cross.
6. Leave: Let a few small dark dabs of the hillside from step 8 peek through. Keep the hillside behind the meadow untouched.
7. Stop when: The lower canvas is a textured green meadow with a ragged grassy edge, sunnier along it, criss-cross marks showing and no single blades yet.

This stage, The cow's shape and horns: This stage builds the cow as a flat shape, gives it a shadow side, and adds its two horns.

## Step 10: Block in the cow
Part of: The cow's shape and horns
Picture: steps/10.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/10.jpg)
Paints:
- Rust brown (#873e20): 2 parts red, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the meadow dry before painting the cow over it.
2. Start: Start with a big upright oval for the body, its middle about 5 in from the left edge. The outline on the Where picture shows the whole shape.
3. Strokes: Add curved pieces: a rounded head with two ear bumps, high on the body toward the loch, and the shaggy back. Curve strokes downward the way hair hangs.
4. Where the paint sits: Paint the whole cow one flat colour, about 7 3/4 in wide and 8 3/4 in tall. No shading or hair yet; this is the base everything sits on.
5. Leave: Let the lowest part of the shape break into ragged points in the grass, so the legs fade into the meadow.
6. Stop when: One flat rust-brown shape stands in the meadow: a shaggy body, a rounded head with ear bumps, a ragged hem. No horns or face.

This stage, The cow's shape and horns: This stage builds the cow as a flat shape, gives it a shadow side, and adds its two horns.

## Step 11: Shade the cow's dark side
Part of: The cow's shape and horns
Picture: steps/11.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/11.jpg)
Paints:
- Dark brown (#563223): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts burnt sienna, 2 parts red
Brush: #10 round
Words:
1. First: Let the cow shape from step 10 dry first.
2. Start: Start on the cow's rump, about 1 1/2 in from the left edge of the canvas.
3. Strokes: Pull long curved strokes down the body, from the spine toward the belly, bending with the round of the back. Curve shorter strokes around the dome of the head and the ears.
4. Where the paint sits: The dark covers most of the body and the rump, which turn away from the sun, and the shadow where the head meets the chest. It also outlines the ear bumps.
5. Leave: Leave the flat base colour showing on the face, on a stripe down the chest, and in streaks between your strokes, so the hair looks layered.
6. Stop when: The cow now looks round and hairy: a dark streaked body, with the warmer face and chest catching the light. No horns or locks yet.

This stage, The cow's shape and horns: This stage builds the cow as a flat shape, gives it a shadow side, and adds its two horns.

## Step 12: Block in the horns
Part of: The cow's shape and horns
Picture: steps/12.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/12.jpg)
Paints:
- Horn cream (#e8d6a5): 12 parts white, 3 parts burnt sienna, 2 parts yellow
Brush: #8 round
Words:
1. First: Let the cow's shading from step 11 dry first.
2. Start: Start where each horn leaves the head, at the two ear bumps, and paint the thick base of each horn first.
3. As in step 10: Building from curved pieces again, as for the cow, but with a small #8 round and two narrow horns.
4. Strokes: Build each horn in curved pieces, thick at the base and narrowing to the tip, bending upward. The longer one reaches up over the hills to a tip about 2 3/4 in from the top edge.
5. Where the paint sits: The horn nearer the left edge is the longer one, about 2 1/2 in tall, sweeping out and up. The other, nearer the loch, is shorter and hooks up at its tip.
6. Leave: Leave the sky and hills showing crisply around each horn; keep the edges clean.
7. Stop when: Two flat cream crescents rise from the head like a wide U, with no shading or dark tips yet.

This stage, The cow's shape and horns: This stage builds the cow as a flat shape, gives it a shadow side, and adds its two horns. By its end: A rounded, dark-streaked brown cow stands in the meadow with two gray-brown curved horns, still with no locks or face.

## Step 13: Shade the horns and darken the tips
Part of: The cow's shape and horns
Picture: steps/13.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/13.jpg)
Paints:
- Horn shadow (#704935): 3 parts burnt sienna, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part red, 1 part white
Brush: #4 round
Words:
1. First: Let the pale horns from step 12 dry first.
2. Start: Start at the base of each horn, where it meets the head, and work out toward the tip.
3. As in step 11: Curved strokes again, as when you shaded the cow, but with a tiny #4 round, following each horn's bend.
4. Strokes: Pull curved strokes along each horn, following its bend from base to tip. Go over the tips a second time so they come out darker.
5. Where the paint sits: Cover almost all of each horn, deepest at the tips, the way old horn darkens. Add one small dab on the face, where the eye hides in the fringe's shadow.
6. Leave: Leave only a thin hint of the pale first coat along the inner curve of each horn; the bright edge comes in the last step.
7. Stop when: The horns read as gray-brown curved horns with dark tips, no longer flat and pale, and a small dark spot sits on the face.

This stage, The shaggy coat: This stage pulls the cow's long locks of hair, light over dark, so the coat looks shaggy.

## Step 14: Pull the ginger locks
Part of: The shaggy coat

- Ginger (#8c4320): 3 parts red, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part yellow
Brush: 1/2 inch angle
Words:
1. First: Let the horns and the cow's shading dry first.
2. Start: Start on the crown of the head between the horns, and pull the first locks down over the face as the fringe.

This stage, The shaggy coat: This stage pulls the cow's long locks of hair, light over dark, so the coat looks shaggy. By its end: The cow is covered in long hanging ginger and honey locks, its eyes hidden by the fringe.

## Step 22: Dry-brush the brightest lights
