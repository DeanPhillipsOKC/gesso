# Lesson: highland cow

The step pictures are in steps/, and each step beside the one before it in pairs/.

## Before you start

You're about to paint a shaggy highland cow standing in tall golden grass at sunset, with violet hills and a glint of loch behind. What makes it sing is the cow's face: the pink nose peeking out of a long, glowing fringe, framed by two curved horns.

The light: The low sun sits behind the cow, near the horizon. It turns the sky gold where it sets, catches the edges of things, and wraps a bright gold rim round the cow's fringe and coat while its face and body stay in warm shadow.

What this painting teaches:
- Dry brush (steps 2, 3, 5, 22): A nearly dry brush skimmed lightly so the paint breaks up on the canvas. It makes the cloud streaks, the shadowed hollows of the hills and the last glowing rim on the cow. Wipe the brush well: too much paint and it goes on solid.
- Thick to thin (steps 13, 14): Press the full width of the angle brush, then lift onto its edge as you pull, so each mark tapers to a point. Every hanging lock of the cow's coat is one of these. Lift at the end, or the locks look blunt and stuck on.
- Flicking (steps 19, 20, 21): A quick upward stroke with the fan brush that lifts off as it goes, so each blade thins at its tip. It builds the tall grass in passes, darkest first. Keep the load light, or the blades turn into flat blobs.

The way there: We start at the back: the sunset sky in one wet blend, then its clouds. The far hills and the loch go on next, then the green hillside, its bushes and the meadow, so the whole background is done fast. The first layers stop short where the next one covers them, so bare canvas showing early on is expected. Most of the time goes on the cow: a flat shape, shading, then two passes of hanging locks before the horns and the face. The tall grass is flicked over the cow's legs after, so it stands in the field. The brightest gold goes on last, when everything it covers is dry.

This stage, The sky: The farthest thing first: a smooth sunset sky with soft cloud streaks across it.

## Step 1: Blend the sunset sky
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/01.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/01.jpg)
Paints:
- Sky blue (#a1a9bf): 6 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna, a touch of yellow
- Pale gold (#c2b1a5): 6 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
- Golden yellow (#fac172): 6 parts white, 3 parts yellow, 2 parts red
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. Start: Start along the top edge of the canvas with Sky blue, stroking side to side from the left edge to the right edge.
2. Strokes: Work down in level strokes. Without rinsing, work in Pale gold next as you go down, then Golden yellow, and stroke back up over each join while wet.
3. Where the paint sits: The blue stays at the top and the Golden yellow sits lowest, running lower on the right side of the sky, where the sun is. A hard line means: add the lighter color and blend again.
4. Leave: The rest of the canvas stays bare until step 4.
5. Stop when: A smooth blue-to-gold sky. Its lower edge is loose and uneven, not ruled: about 3 3/4 in from the top edge for most of its width, about 6 in from the top edge at the right edge.

This stage, The sky: The farthest thing first: a smooth sunset sky with soft cloud streaks across it.

## Step 2: Dry-brush the orange cloud streaks
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/02.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/02.jpg)
Paints:
- Peach orange (#eeb57a): 4 parts white, 1 part yellow, 1 part red, 1 part burnt sienna
- Golden yellow (#fac172): 6 parts white, 3 parts yellow, 2 parts red
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the sky dry completely first.
2. Start: Start about 1 1/4 in from the top edge, skimming a long, flat-bottomed streak of Peach orange across the sky.
3. As in step 1: The Golden yellow from the sky comes back, but now dry-brushed in broken streaks instead of blended smooth.
4. Strokes: Wipe the brush nearly dry and skim sideways with light pressure, so each streak breaks up on the canvas. Lay Golden yellow streaks in among them.
5. Where the paint sits: The streaks stack in loose rows down the sky, more on the right side of the sky, where the sun is low. A few short ones reach the sky's lower edge.
6. Leave: Let the sky show between the streaks and through their broken edges. The rest of the canvas stays bare until step 4.
7. Stop when: Soft orange and gold streaks lie across the sky. If one goes on solid, the brush was too loaded.

This stage, The sky: The farthest thing first: a smooth sunset sky with soft cloud streaks across it. By its end: A blue-to-gold sky with orange and gold streaks lies across the canvas, and the rest of the canvas is still bare.

## Step 3: Light the tops of the clouds
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/03.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/03.jpg)
Paints:
- Pale gold (#c2b1a5): 6 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the cloud streaks dry first.
2. Start: Start near the top edge on the left side of the sky, skimming Pale gold along the upper edge of the highest streak.
3. As in step 2: The same dry brush as the streaks, but with even less paint, and only where the sun catches their edges.
4. Strokes: Use a dry brush with almost no paint and skim sideways along the top of each streak in one light pass.
5. Where the paint sits: Touch only upper edges, where the low sun catches the clouds: a thin line on the highest streaks, and a longer, softer one low in the sky, its top about 3 1/2 in from the top edge.
6. Leave: Leave the orange showing in each streak, and most streaks untouched. The rest of the canvas stays bare until step 4.
7. Stop when: A few streaks have a faint pale glow along their tops. If it reads as new white clouds, you pressed too hard.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: Distance: pale and darker violet hills, shaded so they roll back, with a sliver of water at their foot.

## Step 4: Lay in the far violet hills
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/04.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/04.jpg)
Paints:
- Hazy lavender (#838db6): 4 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna
- Hill violet (#717eab): 3 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the sky dry first.
2. Start: Start with the Hazy lavender range, its rolling tops about 2 1/4 in from the top edge, along the bottom of the sky.
3. Strokes: Bend each stroke down along the curve of each bump. Then lay the Hill violet range in front, its tops a little lower, overlapping the lavender.
4. Where the paint sits: The lavender shows only as the back row of soft tops; Hill violet covers the rest, since nearer land reads darker. Keep both flat.
5. Leave: Small gaps of sky left in the violet are fine; step 5 fills them. The rest of the canvas stays bare until step 7.
6. Stop when: Two flat violet ranges. The lower edge is loose and uneven: about 5 1/4 in from the top edge for most of its width, about 6 in from the top edge at the right edge.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: Distance: pale and darker violet hills, shaded so they roll back, with a sliver of water at their foot.

## Step 5: Shade the far hills' slopes
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/05.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/05.jpg)
Paints:
- Deep violet (#68749e): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts white, 1 part burnt sienna, a touch of yellow
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the violet hills dry first.
2. Start: Start in the middle of the nearer range, about 3 1/4 in from the top edge, dry-brushing Deep violet just under its rolling tops.
3. As in step 2: The same dry brush you used on the clouds, now with the bigger brush, building dark hollows on the hills instead of streaks.
4. Strokes: Skim the nearly dry brush lightly along each slope in a few short passes, building the dark in light layers.
5. Where the paint sits: The dark sits in hollows just under the nearer ridges, most of it on the right side of the far violet hills, with two small patches at the left edge.
6. Leave: Leave the pale back range and the ridge tops showing. The rest of the canvas stays bare until step 7.
7. Stop when: The nearer range has dark hollows, so it reads as hills one behind another, and the gaps from step 4 are filled.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: Distance: pale and darker violet hills, shaded so they roll back, with a sliver of water at their foot. By its end: Two rolling violet ranges sit against the sky with a bright sliver of loch, and the rest of the canvas is still bare.

## Step 6: Lay in the sliver of loch
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/06.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/06.jpg)
Paints:
- Pale gold (#c2b1a5): 6 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
- Hill violet (#717eab): 3 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hills dry first.
2. Start: Start at the right edge of the canvas, about 4 1/2 in from the top edge, at the foot of the far violet hills, with a thin band of Pale gold.

7. Stop when: A bright sliver of loch about 2 1/4 in wide, with a violet shore along its top.

## Step 7: Lay in the green hillside
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/07.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/07.jpg)
Paints:
- Olive green (#737038): 2 parts yellow, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part red, a touch of white
- Gold-green (#a78f33): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hills and the loch dry first.
2. Start: Start at the left edge just below the far violet hills, and run the hillside's curving ridge down toward the loch at the right edge.
3. As in step 6: The same long, level strokes as the loch, now with the big brush across the whole hillside.
4. Strokes: Pull long, level strokes left and right with Olive green, working down band by band to the bottom edge.
5. Where the paint sits: Work Gold-green into the upper slopes and in soft bands across the hillside, where the low sun lies. Keep more Olive green lower down, with a few darker patches.
6. Leave: Leave the far hills and the loch showing. The hillside's top covers the hills' loose lower edge.
7. Stop when: No white canvas shows. The hillside is olive with gold-green bands, its curving top about 3 1/4 in from the top edge at its highest.

This stage, The hillside and the meadow: The ground the cow will stand on: a green hillside dotted with bushes and a glowing meadow base.

## Step 8: Dab the dark bushes on the hillside
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/08.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/08.jpg)
Paints:
- Dark green (#585e43): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part yellow, 1 part white
Brush: old stiff brush
Words:
1. First: Let the hillside dry first, or the dabs turn to mud.
2. Start: Start along the ridge at the left edge, tapping Dark green just under the ridge line, and work along it toward the right edge.
3. Strokes: Tap with the tip of the stiff brush, no water on it. Build each bush as a clump of taps, in rows that slope with the hillside.
4. Where the paint sits: Most bushes sit in rows on the upper hillside, the first row about 3 1/4 in from the top edge, smaller toward the back. A few bigger patches go near the bottom edge.
5. Leave: Leave plenty of the olive and gold hillside showing between the rows and the bushes.
6. Stop when: Dark scrubby patches dot the hillside in rows. Blobs mean the brush is too wet: wipe it on a paper towel.

This stage, The hillside and the meadow: The ground the cow will stand on: a green hillside dotted with bushes and a glowing meadow base.

## Step 9: Dab the sunlit tops of the bushes
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/09.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/09.jpg)
Paints:
- Gold-green (#a78f33): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: old stiff brush
Words:
1. First: Let the dark bushes dry first.
2. Start: Start on the top of a bush in the upper rows on the right side of the bushes on the hillside, tapping a small cluster of Gold-green.
3. As in step 8: The same tapping as the dark bushes, but only a few taps, where the sun catches the bushes.

## Step 10: Blend the meadow base
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/10.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/10.jpg)
Paints:
- Gold-green (#a78f33): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
- Olive green (#737038): 2 parts yellow, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part red, a touch of white
- Dark green (#585e43): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part yellow, 1 part white
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hillside and bushes dry first.
2. Start: Start the back of the meadow about 8 in from the top edge with Gold-green, from the left edge to the right edge.
3. As in step 7: You laid these greens on the hillside in level strokes. Now they cross in X's and run from gold at the back to dark in front.
4. Strokes: Make short criss-cross strokes like overlapping X's, working down. Pick up Olive green for the middle and Dark green near the bottom edge, blending each into the last while wet.
5. Where the paint sits: The meadow glows gold at its back and darkens toward the front. Also lay a thin Gold-green strip of sunlight along the ridge of the green hillside.
6. Leave: Leave the bushes and the hillside showing above the meadow.
7. Stop when: The lower half is a textured meadow, gold at the back, olive in the middle, dark at the bottom, with no grass blades yet.

This stage, The cow's shape: The cow as one big shape, then shaded so it starts to look round and shaggy.

## Step 12: Shade the cow's coat
Part of: The cow's shape
Picture: steps/12.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/12.jpg)

- Dark rust (#5e3223): 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:

## Step 13: Pull the copper locks
Part of: The shaggy coat
Picture: steps/13.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/13.jpg)
Paints:
- Light copper (#9e4d22): 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part red, a touch of white
Brush: 1/2 inch angle
Words:
1. First: Let the shading dry first.
2. Start: Start at the crown of the head, pressing the brush flat and pulling the fringe locks down over the face.
3. Strokes: Press the full width, then lift onto the edge as you pull down, so each lock tapers to a point. Work down the body in overlapping rows.
4. Where the paint sits: Cover the fringe, the top of the head and the whole body with Light copper locks. On the body they hang down and curve with its roundness.
5. Leave: Leave thin gaps of the dark shading from step 12 between locks so they read as hair. Leave the lighter patch on the face and chest alone.
6. Stop when: The cow is covered in long copper locks with dark between them. Blunt ends mean you didn't lift.

This stage, The shaggy coat: The long hanging hair that makes it a highland cow. By its end: The cow is covered in long copper locks with a few bright gold ones, and dark showing between them.

## Step 22: Dry-brush the brightest sunlit edges
Part of: The last lights
Picture: steps/22.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/22.jpg)
Paints:
- Pale gold (#c2b1a5): 6 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
Brush: 1/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let everything dry completely first.
2. Start: Start along the top of the fringe between the horns, dry-brushing short Pale gold strokes that run off the edge of the hair.
3. As in step 3: You skimmed this Pale gold onto the clouds' sunlit edges. Now it goes on the edges the sun hits, with the smallest flat.
4. Strokes: Wipe the brush nearly dry and skim short strokes outward across the edge, so the light looks fuzzy, not drawn.
5. Where the paint sits: Light the rim along the head and back, down the lit side of the chest, and a thin line on top of each horn. Skim a glow across the back of the meadow and tip a few seed heads.
6. Leave: Leave the shaded side of the cow, its face and most of the grass untouched.
7. Stop when: A gold rim glows along the cow's top and lit side, the horns catch a line of light, and the back of the meadow shines.
