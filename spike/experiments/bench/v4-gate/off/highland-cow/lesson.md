# Lesson: highland cow

The step pictures are in steps/, and each step beside the one before it in pairs/.

## Before you start

You're painting a shaggy highland cow standing in tall golden grass at sunset, with violet hills and a glowing sky behind it. The heart of the painting is the cow's face: its pink nose peeking out from a long, sunlit fringe, framed by two curved horns. Keep the mood warm and quiet, like the last light of a summer evening.

The light: The light is a low sun sitting behind the cow, near the horizon. It makes the sky glow gold near the hills, and it catches the edges of the cow's fringe and coat in a bright rim while the face and much of the body stay in soft shadow.

What this painting teaches:
- Dry brush (steps 2, 3, 5, 22): Dry brushing means only the tip of a dry brush goes in the paint, skimmed lightly so the marks break up and let the earlier paint show through. It makes the cloud streaks, shades the far hills and puts the final sunlit rim on the cow. If it goes on solid, the brush was too loaded: let it dry and keep each pass lighter.
- Curved strokes (steps 4, 12, 16, 17): Curved strokes bend with the shape you're painting, which is what makes a thing look round or sloped. They shape the humps of the far hills, the round body of the cow, its horns and its nose. Let every stroke follow the curve, never straight across it.
- Flicking (steps 19, 20, 21): Flicking is a quick stroke up from the base of a blade, lifting off as it goes so each mark thins at its tip. It builds the tall grass in passes, darkest first, and pulls it over the cow's legs so the cow stands in the field. Load the brush lightly, or you'll get flat blobs.

The way there: First you blend the whole sky in one wet go, then dry-brush its warm cloud streaks and light their edges. Next come the far violet hills and a small glowing sliver of loch, kept flat and hazy because they are far away. Then the green hillside, its dabbed bushes and the glowing meadow cover the land, so the cow can be painted straight over them. Most of your time goes on the cow: a flat shape, dark shading, copper and gold locks, then horns and the face. Last, the tall grass is flicked over the cow's legs and the brightest sunlit edges go on once the paint they go over is dry.

This stage, The sky: This stage lays the farthest thing first: a soft sunset sky with warm cloud streaks.

## Step 1: Blend the sunset sky
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/01.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/01.jpg)
Paints:
- Sky blue (#a1a9bf): 6 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna, a touch of yellow
- Pale gold (#c2b1a5): 6 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
- Golden yellow (#fac172): 6 parts white, 3 parts yellow, 2 parts red
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. Start: Start at the top edge with Sky blue in a thin band across the whole width. The sky covers the whole canvas, from the top edge to the bottom edge.
2. Strokes: Pull long side-to-side strokes from edge to edge. Without rinsing, pick up Golden yellow and Pale gold for a broad warm band next, then go back to Sky blue for the rest of the way down.
3. Where the paint sits: The warm band sits high, near the top of the canvas, glowing brightest toward the right edge where the low sun is. Sky blue fills the rest down to the bottom edge, with a few soft warm streaks.
4. Leave: Leave no white canvas anywhere: the sky covers it all.
5. Stop when: Stop when every join is soft and the canvas reads as blue sky with a glowing band near the top.

This stage, The sky: This stage lays the farthest thing first: a soft sunset sky with warm cloud streaks.

## Step 2: Dry-brush the orange cloud streaks
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/02.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/02.jpg)
Paints:
- Peach orange (#eeb57a): 4 parts white, 1 part yellow, 1 part red, 1 part burnt sienna
- Golden yellow (#fac172): 6 parts white, 3 parts yellow, 2 parts red
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the sky dry completely first, or the dry brush will drag wet blue into the streaks.
2. Start: Start in the warm band near the top of the canvas, at the right edge, with Peach orange on a dry brush.
3. As in step 1: Golden yellow again, but this time on a dry brush in broken streaks instead of a wet blend.
4. Strokes: Skim long, flat strokes side to side with light pressure, flat along the bottom and ragged on top. Use Golden yellow for some streaks.
5. Where the paint sits: Most of the streaks sit in the right half of the canvas: a cluster of thin ones in the warm band near the top, and a few longer, ragged ones lower down toward the right edge.
6. Leave: Leave most of the blue showing between the streaks, and let the sky show through their broken edges.
7. Stop when: Stop when soft warm streaks cross the top band and a few broken ones float lower down, none of them solid.

This stage, The sky: This stage lays the farthest thing first: a soft sunset sky with warm cloud streaks. By its end: The whole canvas is blue sky with a glowing gold band and soft, pale-edged cloud streaks drifting across it.

## Step 3: Light the tops of the clouds
Part of: The sky
Picture: steps/03.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/03.jpg)
Paints:
- Pale gold (#c2b1a5): 6 parts white, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the streaks from step 2 dry before you start.
2. Start: Start on the top edges of the cloud streaks in the warm band near the top of the canvas.
3. As in step 2: The same dry-brush skim as the streaks, now with Pale gold and only along the lit edge of each streak.
4. Strokes: Skim Pale gold along the upper edge of each streak with a nearly dry brush, following its long, flat line side to side. Lighten the pressure toward the ends.
5. Where the paint sits: The pale paint rides the top of each streak, where the low sun catches it, and softens the ragged edges into the blue. Give the lower streaks the same light touch.
6. Leave: Leave the warm orange showing in the middle and bottom of every streak, and keep the blue between them.
7. Stop when: Stop when each streak has a soft, pale upper edge and the streaks look blended into the sky rather than stuck on. It should never become a solid area.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: This stage sets the far land against the sky: two hazy violet ranges and a sliver of loch.

## Step 4: Lay in the far violet hills
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/04.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/04.jpg)
Paints:
- Hazy lavender (#838db6): 4 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna
- Hill violet (#717eab): 3 parts white, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part burnt sienna
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the sky and the cloud streaks dry first.
2. Start: Start with Hazy lavender along the rolling top of the back range, its top about 2 1/4 in from the top edge, running from the left edge to the right edge.
3. Strokes: Stroke from the top of each hump down along its curve. Then lay Hill violet as a nearer range overlapping the lower part of the first.
4. Where the paint sits: The pale lavender stays on top as a thin far ridge, and the darker violet fills the rest of the band. The hills are about 4 in tall in all.
5. Leave: Leave the sky and its streaks showing over the top of the hills. A few warm flecks may peep through the nearer range; step 5 covers them.
6. Stop when: Stop when two flat bands of violet run edge to edge under the sky, the back one paler, with soft rolling tops and no shading.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: This stage sets the far land against the sky: two hazy violet ranges and a sliver of loch.

## Step 5: Shade the far hills' slopes
Part of: The far hills and the loch
Picture: steps/05.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/05.jpg)
Paints:
- Deep violet (#68749e): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts white, 1 part burnt sienna, a touch of yellow
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the violet hills dry first.
2. Start: Start just under the rolling top of the nearer range, where the warm flecks show through, with Deep violet on a nearly dry brush.
3. As in step 2: The same dry, light skim as the cloud streaks, now with a wider brush and Deep violet, on the hill slopes.
4. Strokes: Skim short curved strokes that follow the slopes down from each hump, with light pressure, building up in a couple of passes.
5. Where the paint sits: Most of it sits in the right half of the canvas, on the shadowed slopes of the nearer range, with two small touches at the left edge. Cover the warm flecks so the range reads as one shape.
6. Leave: Leave the rolling tops of the nearer range and the whole pale back range untouched.
7. Stop when: Stop when the nearer range has dark, soft-edged slopes under lighter tops, and no warm flecks show through it.

This stage, The far hills and the loch: This stage sets the far land against the sky: two hazy violet ranges and a sliver of loch. By its end: Two rolling violet ranges cross the sky, the nearer one shaded, with a thin glowing strip of loch at the canvas edge and the streaky sky still filling the rest.

## Step 7: Lay in the green hillside
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/07.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/07.jpg)
Paints:
- Olive green (#737038): 2 parts yellow, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part red, a touch of white
- Gold-green (#a78f33): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the hills and the loch dry first.
2. Start: Start at the left edge on the ridge, about 3 1/4 in from the top edge, just below the far violet hills.
3. As in step 6: The same long, level strokes as the loch, now with a big brush and tilted with the slope of the hill.
4. Strokes: Pull long strokes along the slope: the ridge runs down toward the loch, so tilt every stroke the same way, keeping it long and steady. Work down to the bottom edge.
5. Where the paint sits: Lay Olive green over most of it, with Gold-green bands along the upper slope where the sun lies. Let a few darker, smoother patches form lower down.
6. Leave: Leave the far violet hills and the loch showing; the green ridge tucks just under the end of the loch.
7. Stop when: Stop when the green hillside reaches from the left edge to the right edge and down to the bottom edge, with no blue or sky streaks showing through.

This stage, The hillside and the meadow: This stage builds all the land the cow will stand on: the hillside, its bushes and the meadow.

## Step 8: Dab the dark bushes on the hillside
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/08.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/08.jpg)
Paints:
- Dark green (#585e43): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part yellow, 1 part white
Brush: old stiff brush
Words:
1. First: Let the hillside dry first, so the dabs stay crisp.
2. Start: Start along the ridge at the left edge, about 3 1/4 in from the top edge, where the first row of bushes sits.
3. Strokes: Tap with the tip of a nearly dry brush, in rough rows that slope down with the hill toward the loch.
4. Where the paint sits: Most of the bushes sit in the top half of the canvas: three or four rows that follow the hill's curves. Add a few bigger clumps low down, at the left edge and toward the right edge.
5. Leave: Leave the green and gold bands of the hillside showing between the rows, and keep the lower slope mostly clear.
6. Stop when: Stop when ragged dark rows stripe the upper slope and a few clumps sit low down. Blobs mean too much paint: wipe the brush and tap again.

This stage, The hillside and the meadow: This stage builds all the land the cow will stand on: the hillside, its bushes and the meadow.

## Step 9: Dab the sunlit tops of the bushes
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/09.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/09.jpg)
Paints:
- Gold-green (#a78f33): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
Brush: old stiff brush
Words:
1. First: Let the dark bushes from step 8 dry first.
2. Start: Start on the top of a bush in the upper rows, toward the right edge.
3. As in step 8: The same tapping as the dark bushes, but only a few taps on each bush, in Gold-green.
4. Strokes: Tap lightly with the tip of the brush, only a few taps per bush, along its upper edge.

7. Stop when: Stop when a scatter of small sunlit dots sits on the bush tops. If one patch goes solid, stop tapping there.

## Step 10: Blend the meadow base
Part of: The hillside and the meadow
Picture: steps/10.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/10.jpg)
Paints:
- Gold-green (#a78f33): 3 parts yellow, 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine
- Olive green (#737038): 2 parts yellow, 2 parts ultramarine, 1 part red, a touch of white
- Dark green (#585e43): 3 parts ultramarine, 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part yellow, 1 part white
Brush: 1 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the bushes dry first.
2. Start: Start at the left edge about 8 in from the top edge, where the hillside flattens, with Gold-green.
3. As in step 7: Gold-green and Olive green are the hillside's greens, but now you cross them in short X strokes instead of long, level ones.
4. Strokes: Paint short criss-cross X strokes, working down. Pick up Olive green through the middle and Dark green near the bottom edge, crossing strokes over the joins while they're wet.
5. Where the paint sits: The back of the meadow glows gold, the middle is greener, and the front has darker patches. Also brush a thin Gold-green band along the slope between the upper rows of bushes, where the sun lies.
6. Leave: Leave the bushes and their rows showing above the meadow, and let a few small patches of the hillside show through low down.
7. Stop when: Stop when the meadow runs from the left edge to the right edge and down to the bottom edge as a soft, textured blend, with no grass blades yet.

This stage, The cow's shape: This stage builds the cow as one big shape and gives it its shadows.

## Step 11: Block in the cow from pieces
Part of: The cow's shape
Picture: steps/11.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/11.jpg)
Paints:
- Coat rust (#974620): 3 parts red, 2 parts burnt sienna, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of white
Brush: 3/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the meadow dry first.
2. Start: Start with one big oval for the body, low in the meadow. The whole cow is about 7 3/4 in wide, and its top is about 4 1/2 in from the top edge.
3. Strokes: Add a smaller round piece for the head at the top of the oval, on the side nearest the loch. Then add curved ( ) pieces around the body, smaller toward the edges, and fill them in flat.
4. Where the paint sits: Fill the whole shape flat, with no shading. Let the bottom of the body go ragged into the meadow, where the legs will stand in grass.
5. Leave: Leave the hillside and the meadow showing all around the cow, and don't paint the horns yet.
6. Stop when: Stop when a solid, flat cow shape stands in the meadow, body low and head high, with soft, uneven edges.

This stage, The cow's shape: This stage builds the cow as one big shape and gives it its shadows. By its end: A big, round, dark brown cow stands in the meadow, streaked like shaggy hair, with a lighter face and chest.

## Step 12: Shade the cow's coat
Part of: The cow's shape
Picture: steps/12.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/12.jpg)
Paints:
- Dark rust (#5e3223): 1 part red, 1 part ultramarine, a touch of yellow
Brush: 1/2 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let the flat cow shape dry first.
2. Start: Start at the top of the cow's back, on the side farthest from the face, with Dark rust.
3. As in step 4: Curved strokes again, like the humps of the hills, but now pulled down the round body of the cow.
4. Strokes: Pull long strokes down the coat, curving with the round of the body like hanging hair. Make them streaky, not a flat fill, and bend them around the head and fringe.
5. Where the paint sits: Cover most of the body and the fringe with these dark streaks, heaviest down the shadowed side of the body and under the fringe. Keep them off the face and the chest, where light reaches.
6. Leave: Leave the flat mid-brown showing on the face, in a broken strip down the chest, and between the streaks elsewhere.
7. Stop when: Stop when the cow looks shaggy and round, dark nearly all over, with a lighter face and chest.

This stage, The shaggy coat: This stage gives the cow its long, hanging coat in two passes of locks.

## Step 13: Pull the copper locks
Part of: The shaggy coat
Picture: steps/13.jpg (beside the step before: pairs/13.jpg)
Paints:
- Light copper (#9e4d22): 3 parts burnt sienna, 1 part red, a touch of white
Brush: 1/2 inch angle
Words:
1. First: Let the shading from step 12 dry first.
2. Start: Start at the top of the fringe, on the crown of the head, and pull the first locks down over the face.
3. Strokes: Press the full width of the angle brush at the top of each lock, then pull down, lightening onto the edge so it tapers to a point. Curve the locks with the round of the body.
4. Where the paint sits: Cover the fringe and the body with long hanging locks, leaving narrow dark gaps between them. Use fewer on the face and the chest, which stay for later.
5. Leave: Leave the dark streaks from step 12 showing between the locks, and keep the face and chest mostly clear.
6. Stop when: Stop when the coat reads as a mass of copper locks hanging down, with dark showing between them.

This stage, The shaggy coat: This stage gives the cow its long, hanging coat in two passes of locks. By its end: The cow is covered in long copper locks with dark gaps between them and a few small golden glints.

Brush: 1/4 inch flat
Words:
1. First: Let everything dry completely first, so the dry brush skims instead of mixing.
2. Start: Start at the top of the fringe and along the top of the cow's back, where the low sun hits first.
3. As in step 3: Pale gold on a dry brush, as on the cloud edges, now along the cow's sunlit rim with the smallest flat.
4. Strokes: With a nearly dry brush, skim short strokes off the edge of the coat in the direction the hair hangs. Use light pressure and build up in passes.
5. Where the paint sits: Run the rim down the side of the coat nearest the loch. Add a thin line on top of each horn, a soft glow at the back of the meadow and pale tips on some blades.
6. Leave: Leave the shadowed side of the cow, the face and most of the grass as they are.
7. Stop when: Stop when the cow glows with a bright rim on top and down one side, the horns catch a line of light and a few grass tips shine.
