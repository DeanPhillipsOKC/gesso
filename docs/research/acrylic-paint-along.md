# Acrylic paint-alongs for true beginners: researched technique notes

Research for Gesso's lesson generator. Scope: acrylic only, for people who have painted about ten pictures. Written 2026-11-11.

How to read this:

- Claims that go beyond common knowledge carry a numbered footnote. The full source list is at the end.
- A few manufacturer pages (Liquitex, Winsor & Newton) rate-limited the fetcher during research. Where a claim comes from a search-index excerpt of a page rather than a full read, the footnote says so.
- **Sources disagree** boxes mark conflicts, each with a recommendation for a true beginner.
- The last section, **For Gesso**, turns all of this into rules for the lesson generator.

## 1. How hand-made paint-along tutorials are structured

### Step count and length

- Written beginner tutorials from one long-running instructor (Tracie Kiernan, a certified art teacher) run from 3 numbered steps for a single skill like simple clouds[^12], to 7 for a night sky with clouds[^13], to 11 for a full pumpkin scene with background, grass, pumpkin, stem, vine and stars[^20].
- Paint-and-sip studios design a full painting to be finished in one sitting. Adult classes usually run 2 to 3 hours on a 16x20 canvas, with an instructor leading the whole room through each step[^25].
- The design itself is kept simple. Studios pick subjects with bold shapes and little detail, and say no experience is needed[^24][^25].

### Step order

The same order shows up across sources:

1. **Background first, then middle ground, then foreground.** Bob Ross's format, which many paint-along instructors copy, builds nearly every picture in three bands: background, middle ground, then a framing foreground tree or plant[^34]. The pumpkin tutorial goes background, grass, pumpkin base, highlights, shadows and outlines, more highlights, stem, grass texture, vine, stars[^20].
2. **Big shapes and a base layer before detail.** Tutorials start with an underpainting or a flat base and save fine detail for the end[^15][^33]. Putting details in too early is named as a main cause of a painting that looks wrong halfway through[^33].
3. **Within one object, dark to light.** Clouds start as a darker gray-blue or gray-purple base shape, then get a mid highlight, then a near-white highlight on one edge only[^12][^13]. The pumpkin gets an orange base, then a lighter dry-brushed highlight, then dark outlines and a bottom shadow, then a smaller and lighter second highlight[^20]. Each layer covers less area than the one before.

### How instructors phrase steps

- Each step gets a short heading naming the goal ("Paint the dark cloud layer"), then plain instructions, then tips. Kiernan's written steps each have a photo of the result[^13].
- The voice is first person and casual: she says what she did and gives options with labels like "Tip:" and "Optional:", rather than strict rules[^20].
- Instructions tell the learner which brush, which mix, which part of the canvas, and which direction to stroke. For example: stroke down each curve of the pumpkin starting at the top of each bump[^20]; keep highlights on the top-left edges so the light source is the same on every cloud[^12][^13].
- Reassurance is built into the text. The pumpkin tutorial tells learners theirs will look different and that this is fine[^20].

### What they show

- **The result of each step**, as a photo or by camera on the canvas[^13].
- **Brush loading.** Instructors spend real time on how much paint is on the brush, because it decides the stroke. Kiernan tells learners to load only the tip and wipe most off for dry brush clouds, and warns that less is more[^12][^13]. The Art Sherpa has a whole lesson on loading the brush as the biggest new-painter mistake[^38].
- **Test strokes.** Tutorials suggest practicing a stroke on scrap paper first, for example "calibrating" a fan brush on paper before stamping trees[^19], or trying blends on a mixed media pad[^14].
- **On video, real time for the stroke, compressed time for repetition.** James Gurney recommends real-time footage when the viewer needs to see what one stroke does, and time-lapse for long repetitive passages, with narration explaining what was skipped[^35].

### Pacing, waiting and drying

- Paint-and-sip instructors wait for the room to catch up before moving on, and use fast-drying paint and hair dryers so layers and finished pieces dry in class[^25].
- Tutorials tell the learner when to wait: for example, let star spatter dry, or use a blow dryer, before painting the next part[^20]. Kiernan's general advice is to let each layer dry and to work on another area while you wait[^15].
- Watching alone does not teach much. Bob Ross's pace is fast enough that beginners often need to pause and rewind[^34].

### Handling mistakes

- The standard message: acrylic is forgiving. Once an area dries you can paint over it and try again[^24][^22].
- Tutorials name the "ugly stage", the middle of a painting where it looks flat or wrong, and tell learners to keep building rather than stop[^15][^8].
- For a painting that has gone wrong, the advice is: step back or take a photo to see it whole, compare the big shapes to the reference, push the lights and darks apart, merge busy small shapes, and add detail last[^33]. As a last resort, cover it with gesso and start over[^15].

### What the best ones have in common

- One small goal per step, with each step's result shown.
- The brush, the mix, the paint load, the direction of the stroke, and where on the canvas it goes.
- The same light direction on every object.
- Darker values first, then smaller and smaller lighter layers.
- A clear point where the learner waits for paint to dry, and what to do while they wait.
- Permission for the learner's painting to look different.

## 2. Stroke and brush vocabulary

Each entry covers what the stroke leaves on the canvas, which brush to use, how much paint and water, and how to fix or soften it afterward.

### Long horizontal stroke (sky and water blending)

- **Leaves:** Smooth bands of color that soften where they meet.
- **Brush:** A wide flat or a 3/4" wash brush[^14][^16].
- **Load:** An even coat, neither thick nor thin. Start with the darkest sky color and stroke side to side. Without rinsing, pick up the next lighter color, paint below it, then stroke back up over the join several times[^14].
- **Fix:** If a join looks harsh, add more of the lighter color at the join and blend again. To link two very different colors, mix a bridge color from them on the palette (pink and yellow make orange)[^14]. Wipe the brush on a rag when it gets overloaded, and rinse before going into white[^14].

### X stroke, criss-cross, slip-slap

- **Leaves:** Short crossing strokes in two directions. They blend colors on the canvas and leave a soft, slightly textured fill. Smoother than up-and-down strokes for filling a background[^16][^14].
- **Brush:** A flat. Kiernan teaches horizontal and X strokes with a flat for skies and water[^16].
- **Load:** One or two colors on the brush. With a double-loaded brush, how much of each color is on it controls the blend[^14].
- **Fix:** Go over the area with lighter, longer strokes while it is still wet to calm the texture.
- **Note on names:** "Slip-slap" is an informal name some video instructors use for loose crisscross strokes. I found no written source that defines it as a separate technique, so Gesso should treat it as the same stroke as X stroke. Bob Ross also used small X-shaped strokes made with the bristles[^34].

### Double load and triple load

- **Leaves:** A two-tone or three-tone stroke that blends partway on the canvas[^16][^17].
- **Brush:** A flat or angle brush. One corner goes in one color and the other corner in the second color. For a triple load, dip the whole tip in one color and each corner in another[^16].
- **Load:** Reload both colors for each stroke. A touch of water helps the paint flow[^14].
- **Fix:** It is meant to look partly blended. For a smooth gradient, use horizontal blending instead.

### Dabbing and stippling

- **Leaves:** Dots or soft dappled patches. The more you tap one spot, the more solid it gets, so denser dots read darker and can shade a form[^23].
- **Brush:** The tip of a round for small dots[^16]. A stiff, short-bristled stippling brush or an old stiff brush for foliage. A sponge for soft patches, light pressure for soft and firmer for brighter and more textured[^21].
- **Load:** Little paint, fairly dry. A wet brush makes blobs.
- **Fix:** Tap over a too-solid patch with a lighter or darker mix to break it up. Kiernan calls stippling tedious and uses it sparingly in acrylic[^16].

### Tapping or stamping with a fan brush

- **Leaves:** Clusters of short needle or branch marks. With pines, light marks at the top widen to heavier marks at the bottom[^19].
- **Brush:** A fan brush, spread out so the bristles are not clumped[^19].
- **Load:** Thin paint. Kiernan thins black with a little water and warns the technique is hard if the paint is too thick[^19]. Stroke the brush on the palette until it fans out.
- **Motion:** Stamp only the tips, using the middle of the fan, not the side bristles. For a pine, work top to bottom in a zigzag and press harder as you go down. Short strokes in different directions make grass. Dragging down makes waterfalls[^16][^19].
- **Fix:** If the top of the tree is too thick, add fine branches with a tiny round instead of more fan marks[^19]. Common mistakes are thick paint, clumped bristles, stamping with the side, and a top that comes out too heavy[^19].

### Dry brushing

- **Leaves:** Feathery, broken, see-through marks that catch the canvas texture and let the color underneath show[^16][^17].
- **Brush:** Any brush, often a flat or bright[^16]. A stiff brush catches the weave best.
- **Load:** A dry brush with no water. Load only the tip, then wipe most of the paint off on a paper towel. The paint should feel dry and hard to apply[^13][^17].
- **Motion:** Light pressure, skimming the surface.
- **Uses:** Cloud highlights, grass, wood, fences, pumpkin highlights[^16][^17][^20].
- **Fix:** If it went on too solid, the brush was too loaded. Let it dry and dry-brush the base color back over part of it. Build in several light passes rather than one heavy one.

### Scumbling

- **Leaves:** A broken, hazy layer, usually a lighter color over a darker dry layer, with the dark showing through the gaps[^28].
- **Brush:** A dry brush. A stiff bristle works well.
- **Load:** A small amount of paint straight from the tube, with no extra medium[^28].
- **Motion:** Loose strokes in varied directions that follow the form. Mont Marte describes the result as broken and airy, good for clouds and foliage[^21].
- **Fix:** Too much cover means too much paint. Wait until dry and scumble the darker color back in.
- **Sources disagree:** Some sources treat scumbling as a dry-brush stroke, others as dabbing, others as a sponge technique[^28][^21]. For a beginner, Gesso should treat it as "dry brush, but in loose scrubby strokes that follow the shape," and avoid the word unless it is explained.

### Glazing

- **Leaves:** A thin transparent tint over a dry layer. It changes the color underneath without hiding it[^21][^5].
- **Brush:** A soft flat synthetic. Will Kemp uses a #10 flat bright[^5].
- **Load:** A small amount of transparent color thinned with glazing medium. Kemp says water does not work as well, because medium keeps the paint film stable[^5]. Liquitex's glazing card suggests about 1 part transparent color to 10 parts glazing medium, mixed with a palette knife (search-index excerpt)[^10].
- **Which colors:** Transparent pigments such as phthalo blue, quinacridone red, permanent alizarin crimson[^5][^1]. Opaque colors like cadmium red light are better for covering than glazing[^5].
- **Fix:** If a glaze goes too dark, paint lighter opaque color back over it[^5].
- **Beginner note:** Glazing needs medium and transparent paints that most beginners do not own. Gesso should use it rarely, if at all, at this level.

### Wet-on-wet blending

- **Leaves:** Soft transitions between colors with no hard edge.
- **Brush:** A flat or wash brush for large areas[^14].
- **Load:** Do not overload. Less paint often blends better[^17]. If the paint starts to drag, add a tiny bit of water to the brush[^14].
- **Timing:** It has to happen fast, because standard acrylic skins over within minutes (see section 3).
- **Fix:** Streaks in circular or double-load blends often look natural and can be left[^14]. If the paint has started to set, stop blending. Working into tacky paint pulls it up. Let it dry and blend a new layer over it.

### Press and lift (comma, petal and leaf strokes)

- **Leaves:** A teardrop or comma shape, wide where you pressed and pointed where you lifted.
- **Brush:** A round[^32]. Flats and filberts also make petals when you press them flat and pull.
- **Motion:** Touch down with the tip, press so the belly spreads, then pull and lift so the stroke ends on the tip[^32]. A C stroke presses through a curve and releases. A U stroke makes semicircles, petals and the sides of cylinders[^32].
- **Load:** Paint loaded well into the belly, creamy, not runny.
- **Fix:** A blobby stroke means too much paint or too slow a lift. Once it dries, paint background color around the shape to tidy it.

### Thick-to-thin stroke with a flat or angle

- **Leaves:** A line that starts wide and tapers to a point. Used for branches and grass[^16][^17].
- **Motion:** Press the full width for the thick part, then turn onto the edge or tip and lighten pressure. With an angle brush, the lighter you press, the thinner the line[^18].

### Flicking (grass, fur)

- **Leaves:** Short, tapered marks with a broken end.
- **Motion:** A quick stroke upward from the base of the grass, lifting off as it goes, so each mark thins at the top.
- **Load:** Light paint. Too much makes flat blobs.
- **Order:** Lay a dark grass base first, then flick lighter greens and yellows on top so the dark shows between blades. The pumpkin tutorial does a first grass layer early and texture near the end[^20].
- **Sources:** Direct written acrylic sources for flicking were thin. This entry relies on general practice plus the branch and grass guidance above, so treat it as lower confidence.

### Liner work

- **Leaves:** Thin, even lines for branches, outlines, rigging and grass blades[^16][^22].
- **Brush:** A liner or rigger, or the tip of a small round or angle[^18].
- **Load:** Thin the paint with water until it flows. Swirl the brush in water, then in the paint, and add water if the line skips[^18]. Liners need reloading often.
- **Motion:** Hold the brush upright with light, steady pressure. For branches, start thick at the trunk and taper. Let branches fork in different directions and overlap. Wobble the edges slightly so they don't look uniform[^18].
- **Fix:** Liner lines feather or spread if laid into a wet layer. Wait until the area underneath is dry.

### Dotting

- **Leaves:** Round, raised dots. Stars, flower centers, berries, snow[^21].
- **Tool:** The handle end of a brush or a dotting tool[^21].
- **Load:** Dip in fresh paint for each dot or small group. Sources from nail and mandala dotting say re-dipping into skinned or tacky paint gives uneven dots, so keep a fresh puddle. Thicker paint holds a better dot than thin.
- **Fix:** A bad dot can be wiped off while wet, or painted over with background color once dry.

### Splatter (stars, snow)

- **Leaves:** Random speckles.
- **Tool:** An old toothbrush or a flat brush, flicked with a finger or tapped on a handle[^16][^23].
- **Load:** Thinned paint. Too much water gives big drips[^16].
- **Fix:** Wipe unwanted spots right away with a damp cloth or cotton swab. Let it dry before going on[^20].

### Swirls and circular strokes

- **Leaves:** Curved, blended marks. Used for skies around a sun or moon, cloud edges, and abstract backgrounds[^14][^17].
- **Motion:** Circular strokes moving outward from the sun or moon. Load white, then yellow, orange and red without rinsing, wiping excess between colors, and finish with a clean dry brush and white[^17]. For clouds, small circles with the brush tip make a bumpy top edge[^12].
- **Fix:** Soften with lighter passes or, for clouds, by smudging with a finger[^12].

## 3. Acrylic behavior that trips beginners

### Drying time and open time

- In a Golden lab test reported by Will Kemp, a thin brushstroke of standard Golden Heavy Body stayed wet for under 5 minutes, stayed workable for under 10, and was touch dry in under 30. It could not be reworked once set[^4]. Slow-drying Golden OPEN stayed wet 30 to 60 minutes and workable 1 to 3 hours[^4].
- Drying speeds up with warm, dry air, moving air, absorbent surfaces like gessoed canvas, and thin films. It slows with humidity, cold, still air, non-absorbent surfaces, and thick paint[^3]. Hair dryers and fans speed it up[^3][^15].
- **Dry to the touch is not dry through.** A thick layer can feel dry on top and still be wet inside. A rigid board dries only from the front, so it takes longer than canvas[^30]. Golden keeps films under about 1/16 inch because thicker ones stay tacky a long time[^3].

### Painting over wet versus dry layers

- Painting into a layer that has started to set turns it gooey and drags it up. Kiernan says to let each layer dry before the next[^15].
- Lifting also happens when a lower layer was thinned with too much water, which weakens the binder, or when a stiff brush scrubs at it[^30]. The fix is to wait a few hours or overnight on thick passages, keep water to about half the mix or less, and use a softer brush with a lighter touch[^30].
- Wet layers mix: a highlight laid into a wet, very saturated base can vanish into it, so wait longer before highlighting saturated colors[^20].
- Mud comes from mixing too many colors, overblending, painting into a wet layer, or rinsing in dirty water[^36]. Fine lines laid into wet paint spread and soften instead of staying crisp.

> **Sources disagree: wait for dry, or keep it wet?**
> Kiernan says let each layer dry[^15], and Kemp's glazing article says the same, though in comments he notes wet-into-wet gives smoother transitions[^5]. Kiernan's pumpkin lets the first highlight go on before the base is fully dry[^20]. Her sky is blended while still damp[^13].
> **Recommendation:** Blend wet-on-wet only *within* one step, in one area, done in a few minutes. Between steps, the layer should be dry to the touch. For thick paint, or when the next step is fine lines or a contrasting color, say so explicitly and suggest a hair dryer.

### Color shift as it dries

- The acrylic binder looks milky when wet and turns clear when dry, so colors dry darker than they look wet[^1]. Winsor & Newton describes the same binder-driven shift (search-index excerpt)[^11].
- The shift is bigger in dark, transparent and heavily thinned colors and smaller in light, opaque colors (search-index excerpts of Liquitex and Golden material)[^11].
- The fix every source gives: paint a test swatch and let it dry before judging the mix[^1][^22].

### Opacity of common beginner pigments

Golden notes there is no industry standard for measuring opacity. Ratings are relative within a brand, and a transparent pigment can look opaque at full strength from the tube[^2].

| Pigment | Typical behavior | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Titanium white (PW6) | The most opaque white, but one coat rarely covers a dark color | Expect 2 to 3 coats over dark. Use it with little or no water. Student grades have more filler and cover less[^31]. Kemp says a good white is the biggest upgrade for coverage[^7]. |
| Cadmium yellow (and hues) | Opaque in artist grade | Kemp lists cadmium yellow light as having good opacity[^6]. Student "hue" versions use other pigments and vary. |
| Cadmium red (and hues) | Artist cadmium is opaque. Student hues often are not | Liquitex Basics Cadmium Red Medium Hue is listed as translucent (retailer listing, search-index excerpt)[^37]. Kemp uses cadmium red light to cover mistakes[^5]. |
| Ultramarine blue (PB29) | Semi-transparent, as pigment charts rate it | Kemp calls it good opacity for his palette[^6]. Mixed with white it covers well. |
| Burnt sienna | Looks opaque, behaves fairly transparent | Common toning color. Expect extra coats to cover[^29]. |
| Burnt umber | Fairly opaque dark brown | With ultramarine it makes a near-black[^6]. |
| Alizarin crimson (permanent) | Very transparent, looks almost black in the tube | Good for glazes and pinks. A little goes a long way[^6]. |

General rule from Golden: earth colors, cadmiums, cobalts and titanium are dense and opaque. Modern organic pigments (phthalos, quinacridones, Hansa yellow) are transparent and give cleaner, brighter mixes[^1].

> **Sources disagree: is ultramarine opaque?**
> Kemp calls it good opacity[^6]. Pigment charts rate PB29 transparent to semi-transparent.
> **Recommendation:** Tell beginners that pure ultramarine may need two coats over a light or white canvas, and that a mix with white covers much better.

### Retarders and slow-drying products

- Liquitex says to start with up to 25% Slow-Dri in the paint, which slows drying by up to about 40%. More than 25% can keep the paint from drying properly (search-index excerpt of the product page)[^9].
- Kemp recommends no more than about 15% retarder[^7].
- Golden OPEN thinner should not go past 1 part thinner to 3 parts paint[^3].
- Too much retarder leaves a film that stays tacky or soft[^30].

> **Sources disagree: how much retarder?** Liquitex says up to 25%[^9]. Kemp says up to 15%[^7].
> **Recommendation:** Don't require any retarder. A true beginner with craft or student paint won't have it. If it's mentioned, say "a small squeeze, no more than about 1 part to 6 parts paint", which stays under both limits.

### Keeping paint workable on the palette

- Mist the palette lightly with a spray bottle[^22][^14]. Golden warns to use it sparingly, since too much water weakens the paint[^3].
- Use a stay-wet palette (a damp sponge or paper towel under parchment, in a lidded box)[^14][^15][^7].
- Put out bigger puddles. Large piles stay workable longer than small smears[^4].
- Cover the paint when you aren't using it[^3].
- Keep a separate clean water jar for thinning, apart from the rinse jar[^22]. Use a big clear jar so you can see when the water is dirty[^15].

## 4. Making depth and texture with few values

### The core pattern: dark base, then mid, then small light

Every 3D form in these tutorials is built the same way:

1. Lay the whole shape in a darker or mid value.
2. Add a mid-light value over the part that faces the light, leaving the dark visible on the shadow side.
3. Add the lightest value to a smaller area on the very edge that faces the light.

Each step covers less area than the step before[^13][^20]. The light comes from the same direction for every object[^12].

### Clouds

- Base: a gray-blue (white plus a little blue) or gray-purple (roughly 1 part black, 2 parts purple, 3 parts white). Flat bottom, rounded higher top[^12][^13].
- Stroke: small circles with the brush tip, dry brushed with almost no paint, so the cloud stays soft and see-through[^13].
- Mid highlight: about three shades lighter, only on the top edges facing the light. Leave the dark in the middle and at the bottom; that is what makes the cloud look round[^13].
- Top highlight: almost pure white, a tiny amount, on the outermost lit edge. Pushing it slightly outward makes the cloud look taller[^13].
- Distance: clouds near the horizon are smaller, closer together, less defined, and get fewer highlight layers[^12].

### Mountains and hills

- Distant forms are cooler (bluer or grayer), lower in contrast, simpler, and softer-edged. Near forms are closer to their real color, with stronger contrast, sharper edges and more detail[^26].
- Values compress in the distance but keep their order[^26].
- Mountains read best as a few big shapes with a clear light side and shadow side. Beginners tend to over-detail them[^27].
- Warm light usually means cool shadows, and the reverse[^27].
- Practical move: mix the farthest range with the most white and a little blue, then make each nearer range darker and more saturated. Paint back to front so nearer ranges overlap farther ones.

### Fields and grass

- Lay the field as a base gradient, lighter and cooler at the back and darker and greener at the front. Then add texture strokes only in the foreground[^20][^26].
- Texture: a fan brush in short strokes in different directions[^16], or dry brush and flicked strokes, lighter greens over the dark base.
- Keep the far field nearly flat. Texture and detail belong near the viewer[^26].

### Trees

- Pines: stamp with a fan brush from top to bottom, pressing harder lower down so the tree widens[^19].
- Leafy trees: short strokes of analogous greens laid with the side of a flat brush, flipping its angle, plus white added for lighter tints[^17]. Or tap a stiff brush: darkest green first, then mid, then a few light taps on the side facing the light.
- Branches: thin flowing paint with a liner or angle brush, thick at the trunk and tapering, painted in the direction the tree grows[^18].
- Distant trees: smaller, lighter, cooler, with less detail than near ones[^26].

### Round objects (pumpkins, balloons, fruit)

- Stroke direction follows the form. On a pumpkin, every stroke curves down each rib starting at the top of the bump[^20].
- Vary the base color slightly between sections (orange in the middle, orange plus yellow ochre on the sides) so each section separates[^20].
- Highlight: a lighter mix (yellow, orange and white) dry brushed in curved strokes near the top of each section. The bottom quarter stays dark[^20].
- Shadow: a dark brown in the grooves between sections, varied in thickness and broken in places, then dry brushed across the bottom. If the brown looks too harsh, add a little orange to it[^20].
- Second highlight: lighter again, smaller, only on the top of each curve, with a dot of white for shine[^20].
- A cast shadow under the object anchors it to the ground.
- A balloon follows the same plan with one dominant highlight: a curved light patch toward the light, a darker crescent on the far side, and a small near-white shine.

### Underpainting and toned grounds

- Toning means a thin wash of color over the white canvas, such as burnt sienna, yellow ochre, raw umber or ultramarine. It gives a mid value, so lights and darks are easier to judge, and it ties the colors together[^29].
- Burnt sienna suits warm scenes and sits well under blue-heavy subjects like seascapes[^29].
- Let the tone dry fully before painting on it, or the colors muddy[^29].
- Kiernan's general advice is to start with an underpainting and build toward the top layers[^15].
- Kemp warns that the first thin block-in won't fully cover the ground, and isn't meant to[^7].
- In a paint-along, the "background" step often does the job of an underpainting: the whole canvas gets covered first.

### Edges

- Keep edges sharp near the focal point and soften them as forms move away[^33].
- Distant edges are soft. A clean damp brush or a finger can blend them[^26].
- Hard edges everywhere flatten a picture[^33].

## 5. Common beginner mistakes and the fixes instructors give

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too much paint on the brush | Blobs, solid patches where texture was wanted, dry brush that looks flat | Wipe most of the paint off on a paper towel. Load only the tip for dry brush[^12][^13][^17]. |
| Too little paint | Streaky, patchy cover. Kemp sees this often in learners worried about wasting paint[^7] | Put out more paint. Bigger puddles also stay wet longer[^4]. |
| Too much water | Weak, see-through color, paint that lifts on the next pass, longer drying | Keep water at about half the mix or less[^30]. Wipe the ferrule dry[^15]. Use paint thick for cover[^22]. |
| Painting into a half-dry layer | Gooey drag, lifting, muddy color | Let the layer dry, use a hair dryer, or work elsewhere while it dries[^15]. |
| Overblending | Muddy, lifeless color with no texture | Blend a few strokes and stop. Visible streaks often look natural[^14][^36]. |
| Mixing too many colors | Dull mud | Mix two or three colors at most and stop when the color is right[^36]. Use a limited palette[^6]. |
| Dirty water or brush | Colors tinted with the last color | Use a big clear jar and rinse often, with a separate clean jar for thinning[^15][^22]. |
| Details too early | Crowded, busy, the ugly stage | Big shapes and values first, details last, only on shapes that are already right[^33]. |
| Judging wet color | Dried color looks darker than planned | Test a swatch and let it dry first[^1][^22]. |
| One coat of white or a light color over dark | Gray, patchy highlights | Expect 2 to 3 coats. Use the paint thick with little water[^31]. |
| Stamping a fan brush with the side bristles, or paint too thick | Heavy, clumpy trees | Thin the paint a little, spread the fan, stamp with the middle tips[^19]. |
| Uneven lighting | Objects look flat or pasted on | Pick one light direction and put every highlight on that side[^12][^13]. |
| Giving up in the middle | Painting looks wrong partway through | Expect the ugly stage. Step back, photograph or squint, compare to the reference, keep going[^8][^15][^33]. |

## 6. Mixing

### Limited palettes

- Kemp's beginner palette is five colors: titanium white, cadmium yellow light, permanent alizarin crimson, ultramarine blue, burnt umber. He says it covers most realistic painting. A three-tube start is a warm brown, ultramarine and white[^6].
- Burnt umber plus ultramarine makes a near-black that is livelier than tube black[^6].
- A small palette is what teaches mixing. It also stops beginners from making mud by grabbing too many colors[^6][^36].
- Paint-along instructors usually pick a small set and name exact mixes for each step.

### Ratios in tutorials

- Tutorials give rough part ratios. Kiernan's cloud gray is about 1 black, 2 purple, 3 white[^13]. Her pumpkin highlight is about equal parts yellow, orange and white[^20]. Her mid cloud highlight is described as "about three shades lighter" than the base, which is a value step, not a ratio[^13].
- Instructors then say how to adjust: for example, add a little orange if the brown shadow looks too dark[^20].

### Mixing as you go

- Brush-mixing on the palette is normal: pick up a little blue with the white still on the brush to make light blue[^14]. A sunset can be loaded white, then yellow, orange and red without rinsing, wiping excess between colors[^17].
- Kiernan's lighter value for each layer comes from the previous mix plus white, so the colors stay related[^13][^20].

### Lightening and darkening

- To make a tint, add the color to the white a little at a time. A strong color like phthalo blue can overwhelm a big pile of white.
- White makes a color lighter and also duller. Too much turns it chalky or pastel.
- To darken, add the dark into the lighter mix in small amounts.
- Burnt umber dulls a too-bright color, for example softening a bright orange[^6].

### Keeping colors clean

- Mix on the palette, not on the canvas[^22].
- Keep two water jars: one for rinsing, one clean for thinning[^22].
- Wipe the brush on a rag between colors. Rinse fully before going into white[^14].
- Mixing opaque earth and mineral pigments together gives duller mixes. Transparent modern pigments mixed together stay bright[^1].
- For bright mixes, pick colors that lean toward each other. A red-leaning yellow plus a yellow-leaning red gives the brightest orange[^6].
- Set the palette up the same way every time so colors are easy to find and don't get dragged into each other[^36].

## 7. Flower masses and clumps in a field

Covers lavender rows, poppies, wildflower meadows, lupines and flower bushes such as hydrangeas. Added 2026-11-11 after lesson runs had no guidance for these.

### Paint the ground first, flowers on top

- Lay the field or background first and let it dry, then paint the flowers over it. Mont Marte says this avoids the mess of cutting the background in around each flower[^48]. Its poppy field lays a dark green and crimson base over the whole foreground and lets it dry before any poppy goes on[^47].
- Flowers go dark to light, in thin layers rather than one heavy coat[^48].
- Kiernan's lavender field starts the field as a solid deep violet sloping up to the horizon, with white blended in at the top so the back is lighter and the front darker[^39].

### Building a clump in passes

The tutorials agree on the order, even when the subject changes:

1. **Stems and leaves (where the flower has them).** Lupines start with tall, thin stems in a very dark green (about equal parts green and dioxazine purple), then narrow pointed leaves pressed out from the tip to a wider base[^41]. Debasree Dey's poppy field paints liner-brush stems before the poppy heads (search-index excerpt)[^61]. Mont Marte's poppies do the opposite and add stems last with a fine round[^47]. A painted lavender sprig drags the stems first with a flat, then adds leaves with chisel-edge strokes[^46].
2. **Dark base of the bloom.** A solid coat of the darkest flower color. Hydrangea heads start as solid dark balls of phthalo blue, dioxazine purple or red[^42]. Lavender rows in the Tuscan scene start as dioxazine purple with a little black[^40]. Lupines get dark purple oval marks angled out from the stem, narrow at the top and wider lower down[^41].
3. **Mid value.** The same color plus white, over most but not all of the dark. Kiernan's lavender rows stamp a purple, blue and white mix that must read lighter than the base, and she says to add white if it doesn't[^39].
4. **Light tips.** A smaller amount of a much lighter mix, mostly on the top or lit side. Lavender gets a double load of purple and white, then a tiny bit of white only in the back rows[^39]. Lupines get a pale pink double load of magenta and white[^41]. Hydrangeas get pure white marks last, sparingly[^42].

What every one of these repeats: don't cover the dark. Kiernan's lupine step warns against covering all of the dark purple layer, and she says the light-over-dark layering is what creates depth[^41]. Her hydrangeas keep the bottom of each ball dark and say not to put too much light purple there[^42]. Marks get dense at the top of a bush and sparse at the bottom[^42].

### Marks, brushes and paint load

| Mark | Used for | Brush and load | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stamp with the brush end or edge | Lavender rows, vine rows, the dark shadow at the base of each row | A #12 bright, stamped straight down. Black on the edge for row shadows | [^39][^40] |
| Short upward drag or dab | Lavender texture, foreground clusters | A bright or the corner of a flat or fan brush, tapped or dragged up a little | [^40][^44] |
| Dotted columns | Near lavender spikes | A #4 round, dots in a column, then lighter dots on top | [^40] |
| Overlapping columns of short strokes | Lavender spikes in a meadow | A round, double-loaded purple and white. Vary the angle of each column and how much of each color is reloaded | [^43] |
| Oval marks angled out from a stem | Lupine and other spike flowers | A #4 round. Narrow ovals at the top, wider lower down | [^41] |
| Small four-petal marks | Hydrangea heads | A #4 round, angle changed often, some marks in clusters of two or three, some going past the outline for ruffled edges | [^42] |
| Dabs and spots | Poppy field color, poppy centers, far blooms | A filbert or round. Dabs of color through the middle ground, a dark dab off-center in each poppy, a white dot in the center | [^47] |
| Plain dots | Far wildflowers, small hydrangeas | The tip of a round. Simple dots replace petal shapes on small or distant flowers | [^42][^43] |

- Load is wet and creamy for stamps and ovals. Kiernan wipes the brush without rinsing between the dark and light passes on lavender, so the colors stay related[^39].
- The same stroke repeated many times can be done on a still-wet layer. Kiernan says the lupine magenta doesn't need the purple to dry first[^41]. Detail marks over a base do need it dry: her hydrangea balls must dry before the petal texture goes on, and Mont Marte waits for the poppy base to dry before the scarlet highlight[^42][^47].
- Kiernan notes the paint darkens as it dries, so a dried hydrangea may need another light layer[^42].
- Stippling with a stiff brush shows up for foliage and land in these tutorials, not for the flowers themselves[^43][^39].

### Size and detail shrink with distance

- Far flowers are smaller, closer together, lighter, and less detailed. Near flowers are larger, more spread out and more detailed. Kiernan's white wildflower dots are bigger and wider-spaced at the bottom of the canvas and smaller and packed tighter farther back[^43]. Her far vine rows have white added and are smaller and closer together, and each nearer row is larger and spaced a bit wider[^40].
- Distant lavender rows are a muted purple and gray-blue. Near rows are deeper and more saturated[^44].
- Detail drops out first. Small or far hydrangeas become dots, far leaves become simple brush marks, and vein lines go only on the big near leaves[^42]. Very light purple dots stand in for lavender far behind the cypress trees[^40].
- This matches the general distance rule in section 4: cooler, paler and softer far away, warmer and sharper up close[^26].

### Rows in perspective

- Rows aim at one point on the horizon. A one-point perspective field guide puts a dot on the horizon line and draws diagonal lines from it out to the sides and bottom of the paper, so each row is a long triangle[^45].
- Each row is narrow at the back and wide at the front. Kiernan's lavender rows are wider in front and narrower toward the back, the rows on the right tilt right and the row on the left tilts left[^39]. Her vine rows are thin and pointed far away and wide at the bottom of the hill[^40].
- Spacing grows toward the viewer: tight at the top, wider at the bottom[^44][^40].
- Thin warm strips of ochre or orange between rows suggest the soil and keep the field from looking flat[^44].
- Kiernan's dark accent between the rows stops about three-quarters of the way back and fades out[^39]. The far rows stay simple.
- Kiernan tells learners to "Think abstract" for lavender rows[^39]. The rows read as stamped stripes of color, not individual plants.

> **Sources disagree: draw the guide lines, or just paint the rows?**
> The perspective guide draws every line from a vanishing point first[^45]. Kiernan never mentions a vanishing point. She gets the effect from row width, tilt and spacing alone[^39][^40].
> **Recommendation:** For a beginner, have them mark one dot on the horizon and draw three to five faint chalk or pencil lines fanning out from it before painting the rows. It costs a minute and stops rows from running parallel or crossing.

### Common beginner mistakes with flower masses

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Covering all of the dark base | A flat, one-value blob with no depth | Light marks go mostly on top and on the lit side. Leave dark showing at the bottom and between marks[^41][^42]. |
| Cutting the background in around flowers | Messy halos and gaps around each bloom | Paint the field first, let it dry, then paint flowers over it[^48]. |
| Same size and spacing everywhere | A wallpaper pattern with no depth | Smaller, tighter, lighter in the back. Bigger, wider-spaced, more detailed in front[^40][^43]. |
| Parallel rows | Rows look like a flat striped wall | Aim every row at one horizon point, wide in front and narrow in back[^45][^39]. |
| Petal detail on distant flowers | A busy, flat background | Use dots or stamped color far away. Save petal shapes for the front[^42]. |
| Detail marks on a wet base | Marks sink in and blur | Let the base of the bloom dry before petal texture or highlights[^42][^47]. |
| Flower color bleeding into the hill or sky | Purple smeared into green | Let it dry, then repaint the edge with the hill color[^39]. |

## 8. Water reflections

Covers still and gently moving water: lakes with trees, a boat, a sun or moon path.

### What a reflection is

- A reflection sits directly below the object, on the side toward the viewer[^60].
- Light things reflect a little darker. Gurney's examples are clouds, white houses and light leaves. He explains it this way: some of the light goes into the water instead of bouncing off[^56].
- Draw Paint Academy's rule is that values squeeze toward the middle: the darks reflect slightly lighter and the lights slightly darker[^57].
- The reflection is softer and more broken than the object. Kiernan paints a land reflection lighter and more see-through than the land itself[^50], and her pine reflection doesn't have to be as detailed as the tree[^49]. Penny Apple Studio says reflections are like a mirror but slightly distorted, and that making them too perfect is a mistake[^55].
- Reflections are most solid right under the object and break up as they move away from it (watercolor source)[^59].
- Water mirrors best when you look across it at a shallow angle. From higher up, the reflection gets weaker[^56].
- Jenna Rainey mixes reflection colors slightly darker and cooler than the sky and land, and keeps the water's color temperature matched to the sky: warm water under a sunset, cool water at midday (watercolor source)[^58].

### Order of painting

The order across the acrylic tutorials:

1. **Everything above the water first.** Penny Apple Studio paints the trees, rocks or sky first and then pulls those colors down into the water[^55]. Rainey lets the landscape dry completely before starting the water[^58].
2. **The water base.** Kiernan's lake base is about 3 parts white to 1 part cerulean, in loose left and right strokes with a 3/4" flat, not fully blended, with a few darker streaks left in for ripples[^49]. The rowing boat lesson blocks in the water but leaves the area that reflects the sky clear, then paints that area light blue[^54]. Penny Apple Studio starts with the dark areas of the water, then lays lighter reflected light on top[^55].
3. **Reflection colors in vertical strokes.** Kiernan wipes a #12 bright loaded with black until only a little paint is left and strokes down just below each pine[^49]. In another scene she pulls thin, dry vertical strokes down below the land, then dry-brushes white verticals below the aspen trunks[^50]. Draw Paint Academy drags the subject's main color down through the water with a large brush for glassy water[^57]. For a moon path, Kiernan paints vertical white lines straight below the moon[^52].
4. **Horizontal ripple dashes over the verticals.** Kiernan paints thin horizontal lines of white and light blue-violet over the vertical strokes with a round, then loose horizontal lines across the whole lake[^50]. The rowing boat lesson uses the cutting edge of a flat for short horizontal flicks[^54]. Penny Apple Studio puts ripples last, dragging a lightly loaded brush sideways[^55].
5. **Thin lines and sparkles once dry.** A white line where the water meets the land, with a #4 round[^50]. Sparkles beside the moon path as tiny asterisks with a 10/0 liner[^52]. Kiernan's sun path waits until everything is dry before the second, brighter layer[^51].

### Stroke direction

- **Vertical** for the reflection of anything upright: trees, trunks, a boat, the land at the shore[^49][^50][^57].
- **Horizontal** for the water surface, ripples, and the sky reflected in open water[^49][^54][^55].
- **Zigzag** for straight upright lines like a mast or boat planks, since ripples bend them[^54].
- **Sun and moon paths** have been painted both ways. Kiernan's sunset path is horizontal left and right strokes under the sun[^51]. Her moon path is vertical lines below the moon[^52]. Both get wider toward the bottom of the canvas[^51][^52].
- Ripple lines near the horizon are short and close together. Lower down they get longer and more spread out[^49].

> **Sources disagree: vertical or horizontal for the reflection itself?**
> Rainey says reflections follow the water's surface, so strokes are horizontal only (watercolor)[^58]. Kiernan, Draw Paint Academy and Brienne Brown pull the reflection down first, then add horizontals[^49][^50][^57][^59].
> **Recommendation:** Vertical pull-downs for the reflection of trees, a boat, or the shore. Horizontal for the water base, the sun or moon path, and every ripple. The horizontal dashes laid over the verticals are what make it read as water.

### Softening and broken edges

- Dry-brushing does most of the softening. Kiernan's reflections use a thin, wiped brush, so the strokes go on broken and see-through[^49][^50].
- Penny Apple Studio drags a dry brush over wet paint to blur edges, and breaks up a reflection with quick strokes so it isn't too exact[^55].
- Brienne Brown lifts a few horizontal lines out of a dry reflection with a clean, damp brush (watercolor technique; in acrylic the equivalent is painting a few water-colored horizontal dashes across it)[^59].
- Leaving the ground showing through gives a broken, see-through reflection. Kemp varies the paint thickness so more or less of the sand shows[^53].
- A boat reflection goes on lightly, and the rowing boat lesson tells learners not to be too accurate because the water is moving[^54].
- Kiernan pushes distant tree reflections toward gray as loose horizontal marks, since they're farther away[^50].

### Drying between layers

- Let the scene above the water dry before working the water[^58].
- Let each layer of the water dry before the next. Penny Apple Studio says this keeps reflections clear[^55]. Kiernan's sun path waits for full dryness before its second, brighter layer, and paints the wave between the two layers so the second layer can go around it[^51].
- Fine ripple lines, the waterline and sparkles need a dry surface, the same as any liner work (section 2).
- The vertical pull-down and the horizontal dashes can go into a damp base when the goal is a soft reflection. Draw Paint Academy's glassy water and Brown's soft reflections both work wet[^57][^59]. For a beginner with fast-drying acrylic, it is safer to let the verticals dry and then add the horizontals.

### Keeping the reflection behind the object

- Kemp glazed his boat's reflection darker with raw umber, a little ultramarine and white, because it was competing with the boat. He wanted the boat to stay the focus[^53].
- The rowing boat lesson uses a very light blue splash at the water's edge to separate boat from water[^54].
- Kiernan paints a white line where water meets land[^50].

### Common beginner mistakes with reflections

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Reflection as sharp and detailed as the object | Looks like a mirror or a second picture | Fewer details, softer edges, a thin dry brush[^49][^55]. |
| Reflection at the same value as the object | Flat, no sense of water | Lights a bit darker, darks a bit lighter[^56][^57]. |
| Reflection offset from the object | Looks pasted on | Put it straight below the object, starting at the waterline[^60]. |
| Ripple lines the same length everywhere | Water looks like a flat wall | Short and close near the horizon, longer and wider apart lower down[^49]. |
| Ripples on wet paint | Lines blur into the base | Let it dry before thin lines[^51]. |
| Reflection louder than the subject | Eye goes to the water, not the boat | Knock it back with a darker or grayer layer[^53]. |
| Overworking | Muddy, fussy water | A few confident strokes. Don't copy every color[^55][^58]. |

## For Gesso

Rules a lesson generator should follow. These assume standard (not slow-drying) student or craft acrylics, a canvas or canvas panel, and a learner who has painted about ten pictures.

### Step structure

1. Write 6 to 12 steps for a full picture. Use 3 to 5 for a single object or skill drill. Each step does one job in one area of the canvas.
2. Order: background, then middle ground, then foreground. Within each object: base shape, then shadow and mid values, then highlights, then details. Fine details, outlines, splatter and dots come last.
3. Give each step a short title naming the goal ("Paint the base of the far hills"), then the instruction, then at most one or two tips.
4. Every step names: the brush (type and rough size), the mix (colors and rough parts), the paint load (wet and creamy, thin and flowing, or nearly dry), the stroke, the direction, and where on the canvas.
5. Say what the step should look like when done, including what should still be showing ("leave the darker gray visible in the middle").
6. Start the lesson by stating the light direction and keep every highlight on that side.
7. Include a reassurance line at most once or twice per lesson ("yours will look different, and that's fine"), and name the ugly stage once, around the middle.
8. Suggest a test stroke on scrap paper before any new stroke: fan brush stamping, liner lines, dry brush, press and lift.

### Layering and drying

1. Wet-on-wet blending happens only within one step, in one area, and has to be done in about 5 minutes. Tell the learner to work fast and to stop blending as soon as the paint starts to drag.
2. Between steps, the layer should be dry to the touch. Mark these points with an explicit wait: "Let this dry (a few minutes, or use a hair dryer on low) before the next step."
3. Always require a dry layer before: fine liner lines, dots, outlines, a contrasting color over the area, or dry brush highlights over a saturated base.
4. Never tell the learner to scrub or re-brush an area that has started to set. Say: let it dry, then paint over it.
5. Light over dark needs 2 to 3 coats. Say so, and wait between coats.
6. Keep paint layers thin to medium. Don't call for thick impasto in a beginner lesson.
7. Thinning: for normal painting, use little or no water. For liner work and splatter, add water until it flows like ink. Never more than about half water in any mix.
8. Don't require retarder, glazing medium or slow-drying paint. If mentioned as optional, keep retarder under about 1 part to 6 parts paint.
9. Mention once per lesson: colors dry a little darker, so test a mix on the palette edge before a big area.

### Values and texture

1. Every 3D object uses at least three values, each covering less area than the last: a darker base, a mid value on the lit side, a small lightest accent on the lit edge.
2. Make values by adding white to the previous mix (or the dark into it) so they stay related. Name them dark, mid, light.
3. Distance: farther objects get more white, a little more blue or gray, less contrast, softer edges and no texture. Nearer objects get darker darks, warmer color, sharper edges and texture.
4. Texture (grass blades, foliage taps, bark) only in the middle ground and foreground.
5. Stroke direction follows the form: curved down the ribs of a pumpkin, around a balloon, in small circles for clouds, upward for grass, outward from the trunk for branches.
6. Ground each object with a cast shadow or a darker patch underneath.
7. Keep the sharpest edges and strongest contrast at the focal point. Soften elsewhere.

### Flower masses

1. Paint the field or ground under the flowers first and let it dry. Never ask the learner to paint around flowers.
2. Build every clump in this order: stems and leaves in a very dark green (if the flower has them), dark bloom base, mid value (base plus white) over most of it, light tips on the top or lit side only. Each pass covers less than the last.
3. Every flower pass says what dark must stay visible: "leave the dark purple showing at the bottom of each bush."
4. The petal texture and highlights go on a dry base. A second color in the same pass (magenta into purple) can go on wet.
5. Name one mark per flower type and keep it the same through the lesson: stamps for lavender rows, ovals in a column for spikes, small four-petal marks for hydrangea heads, dabs for poppies, dots for far wildflowers.
6. Scale by distance in three bands. Back: tiny dots or stamps, close together, paler and grayer, no petals. Middle: larger marks, more space between. Front: largest, widest-spaced, with petal shapes, centers and a few stems.
7. For rows in perspective: mark one dot on the horizon and draw three to five faint chalk lines fanning out from it before painting. Rows are narrow and tight at the back, wide and spaced out at the front.
8. Let the dark between rows fade out about three-quarters of the way back. Far rows get no light tips.
9. Optional: a thin warm ochre or orange strip between near rows for soil.
10. Tell the learner to think of rows and bushes as stamped color, not individual flowers, and that the dark gaps are what make them look full.

### Water reflections

1. Finish and dry everything above the water before starting the water.
2. Order: water base in horizontal strokes, reflection colors pulled down in vertical strokes, horizontal ripple dashes across them once dry, then thin lines (waterline, sparkles) last.
3. Put every reflection directly under its object, starting at the waterline.
4. Mix the reflection from the object's own colors, a little darker for light objects and a little lighter for dark ones. Never the exact same mix. Push distant reflections toward gray.
5. Reflections use a thin, wiped brush and less detail than the object. Say "softer and less detailed than the tree" in the step.
6. Vertical strokes for trees, trunks, boats and shore. Horizontal strokes for the water base, the sky in the water, the sun or moon path, and all ripples. Zigzags for masts and posts.
7. A sun or moon path is narrow under the light and wider toward the bottom of the canvas, brightest in the middle. Paint it in two layers, the second lighter and smaller, with a dry wait between.
8. Ripple dashes: short and close near the horizon, longer and wider apart near the viewer.
9. If the reflection pulls the eye from the subject, add a step to knock it back with a slightly darker or grayer mix.
10. Mark an explicit dry wait before the horizontal dashes and again before any liner lines or sparkles.

### Stroke results (stroke: leaves, then)

- **Long horizontal blend (flat, even coat):** leaves smooth bands of color. Then: stroke back over each join while wet. If a join is harsh, add more of the lighter color there.
- **X stroke / criss-cross / slip-slap (flat):** leaves a soft, slightly textured blended fill. Then: go over it with lighter, longer strokes while wet to calm it, or leave it as texture.
- **Double load (flat or angle, two colors on two corners):** leaves a two-tone stroke that blends partway. Then: reload both colors each stroke. Don't try to make it fully smooth.
- **Dabbing / stippling (round tip or stiff brush, little paint):** leaves dots or dappled patches that get more solid the more you tap. Then: tap a lighter mix on the lit side and leave the darker taps showing underneath.
- **Fan brush tapping (thin paint, spread bristles, middle tips):** leaves needle or branch clusters, light at the top and heavier where pressed. Then: if the top is too heavy, add fine branches with a small round. Don't add more fan marks.
- **Dry brush (no water, tip loaded, wiped nearly clean, light pressure):** leaves feathery, broken, see-through marks. Then: build in several light passes. If too solid, let it dry and dry-brush the base color back over part of it.
- **Scumbling (dry brush in loose scrubby strokes, light over dark):** leaves a hazy, broken layer with the dark showing through. Then: if it covers too much, let it dry and scumble the dark back in.
- **Glazing (transparent color plus medium, over dry paint):** leaves a see-through tint. Then: if too dark, paint lighter opaque color over it. Use rarely at this level.
- **Wet-on-wet blend (two wet colors, light load):** leaves a soft transition. Then: stop when the paint drags. Streaks can stay. If it has set, let it dry and blend a new layer on top.
- **Press and lift (round, press the belly, lift to the tip):** leaves a teardrop or comma for petals and leaves. Then: if blobby, use less paint and lift faster. Tidy edges with background color once dry.
- **Thick-to-thin (flat or angle, press then turn to the edge):** leaves a tapered line for branches and grass. Then: add a few thinner offshoots with a liner.
- **Flicking (light load, quick upward stroke from the base):** leaves tapered blades with broken tips. Then: flick a lighter green over the dark base, fewer as you go up.
- **Liner (paint thinned to ink, brush upright, light steady pressure):** leaves thin flowing lines. Then: reload often. Only paint lines over dry paint, or they spread.
- **Dotting (handle end or dotting tool, fresh paint each dot):** leaves round raised dots. Then: wipe bad dots off while wet, or cover with background color once dry.
- **Splatter (thinned paint, toothbrush or flicked brush):** leaves random speckles. Then: wipe strays right away with a damp swab. Let dry before the next step.
- **Small circles with the brush tip (clouds):** leaves a bumpy, soft edge. Then: smooth with a finger or the same circular motion, and highlight only the lit top edges.
- **Stamp with the brush end (bright or flat, creamy load, straight down):** leaves a short, blunt block of color, a strip of them making a lavender or crop row. Then: stamp a lighter mix over the same row without covering all of it, and wipe (don't rinse) between dark and light passes.
- **Dotted or oval column (round, creamy load, marks angled out from a stem):** leaves a spike of small marks, narrow at the top and wider at the base (lavender, lupine). Then: add a lighter column over it, mostly near the top, leaving the dark showing lower down.
- **Small petal clusters (round, angle changed each mark, some past the outline):** leaves a ruffled, textured flower head (hydrangea, lilac). Then: put the lighter marks dense at the top and sparse at the bottom. Only on a dry base.
- **Far flower dots (round tip, small and close together):** leaves a speckled meadow that reads as distant flowers. Then: make the dots bigger and wider apart lower on the canvas, and give only the nearest ones petal shapes.
- **Vertical pull-down (bright or flat, wiped nearly dry, stroked straight down from the waterline):** leaves a soft, streaky, see-through reflection of a tree, boat or shore. Then: once dry, cross it with thin horizontal dashes of the water color.
- **Horizontal ripple dash (cutting edge of a flat or a small round, light load, side to side):** leaves thin broken lines that read as a water surface. Then: keep them short and close near the horizon and longer and farther apart lower down.
- **Zigzag (small round, light load):** leaves a wavy vertical line for the reflection of a mast, post or plank. Then: break it in one or two places with a ripple dash.
- **Sparkle (10/0 liner, white, after everything is dry):** leaves a tiny asterisk of light on the water. Then: use only a few, beside the brightest part of the sun or moon path.

### Mixing

1. Use a limited palette of 4 to 6 colors for the whole lesson, always including titanium white and a dark (burnt umber plus ultramarine works as a near-black).
2. Give mixes as rough parts ("about 3 white to 1 blue") plus what it should look like ("a pale sky blue").
3. To lighten, add color to white a little at a time. To darken, add the dark into the mix a little at a time.
4. Build each object's dark, mid and light from one base mix by adding white, so the values match.
5. No mix uses more than three colors besides white.
6. Remind the learner: mix on the palette, not the canvas; wipe or rinse between colors; rinse fully before white; keep a clean water jar for thinning.
7. Ask for enough of a big-area mix to finish the area, so it doesn't run out or dry mid-step, and suggest a light mist of the palette if it's skinning over.
8. Expect transparent pigments (alizarin, student cadmium red hues, phthalos, burnt sienna) to need two coats over a light canvas. Mix in a little white if a color must cover in one coat.

### Gaps worth a follow-up pass

- Liquitex and Winsor & Newton technical pages rate-limited the fetcher. Re-read [^9], [^10] and [^11] directly before relying on the exact numbers (25%, 40%, 1:10).
- There's no written source for "slip-slap" as a distinct stroke, and only thin acrylic-specific written sources for flicking grass and for dotting consistency. Those entries lean on general practice.
- The Art Sherpa's teaching is mostly video. A transcript pass on two or three of her beginner lessons would add phrasing and pacing data from the most-watched paint-along instructor.
- No controlled research was found on paint-along formats specifically (close-ups, step length, learner outcomes).
- Sections 7 and 8: no paint maker had a technical page on flower fields or water reflections (Golden, Liquitex, Winsor & Newton and Daler-Rowney searches came up empty), so these lean on instructor tutorials. Three reflection sources are watercolor ([^58], [^59], [^60]) and were used only for principles, not handling. Painters Online (Wendy Jelbert, Terry Harrison) and Kevin Hayler's water guide refused the fetcher and are worth a retry. No written source covered flicked spikes as a separate flower mark, so that stroke result is left out.
